§3   Teams & Team Processes

Summary

Teams are the primary vehicle through which organizations implement quality improvement. This chapter covers the definition and benefits of teams, the eight recognized team types, Tuckman's five stages of development, KESAA member selection, the seven team roles, facilitation processes, consensus decision-making, groupthink prevention, and team performance measurement.

3.1 Definition and Benefits

Definition

A team is a group of individuals performing interdependent tasks toward a common mission. Teams differ from mere groups in their degree of interdependence and shared accountability.

Teams vary by: number of members, frequency of meetings, leadership type, range and complexity of skills required, and total working time together.

Why Teams Fail in the United States

  • Cultural emphasis on individual achievement over collective outcomes
  • Reward systems that reinforce individual rather than team performance
  • Lack of organizational systems to support teams
  • Failure to prepare managers for changing roles
  • Inadequate recognition and compensation structures for team contributions
  • Incomplete understanding of group dynamics
  • Impatience of top management with the time required for team maturation

Benefits of Effective Teams

CategorySpecific Benefit
ProcessSynergistic process design; broader knowledge of business processes
Problem-solvingObjective problem analysis; greater innovation; more flexible response to change
FinancialReduced costs and turnover
HumanEnhanced ownership, commitment, and development of new leadership skills

3.2 Eight Team Types (BoK I.C.1)

TypeStructure & PurposeKey Characteristics
Process Improvement Cross-functional; guided by a defined improvement plan; breakthrough-level changes Leader selected by management; has sponsor; meets regularly
Work Groups (Natural Teams) Same department; monitor and improve their own processes on an ongoing basis Empowered; report to department supervisor; intimate process knowledge
Self-Managed (Self-Directed) Day-to-day operational authority; members control scheduling, assignments, and processes Best success on greenfield sites; requires significant training and organizational support
Temporary / Ad Hoc Formed for a specific problem or event; disbands upon completion Examples: ISO audit prep, emergency quality issue, customer complaint investigation
Cellular Production cells (often U-shaped); operators perform sequential steps to produce a part or product Effectiveness depends on coordination, timing, and inter-member cooperation
Virtual Members in different locations; connected by technology (video, chat, shared databases) Selected for competence, not location; key challenge is building trust without face-to-face contact
Special Project Major organization-wide efforts: Baldrige applications, mergers, new IS implementations, reengineering Members may be drawn from across the company; often time-bounded
Steering Committee Top management group that initiates, guides, resources, and monitors all other teams Every team should have a link to the steering committee; removes barriers and sets priorities

3.3 Tuckman's Stages of Team Development (BoK I.C.2)

StageNameCharacteristic BehaviorsLeader's Role
1 Forming Members are proud to be selected but anxious. Polite, cautious, testing boundaries. Each brings own identity, values, and priorities. Define roles, performance expectations, and acceptable behaviors
2 Storming Individualistic thinking dominates. External loyalties create tension. Subgroups form. Leadership may be challenged. Task reality becomes apparent. Manage conflict; prevent team from disbanding; keep focus on mission
3 Norming Shift from personal concerns to team orientation. Competitive attitudes give way to cooperation. Cohesiveness and ground rules emerge. Reinforce collaborative norms; facilitate trust-building
4 Performing Team is mature, smooth, and cohesive. Members collaborate effectively, achieve goals, and continuously improve. May not be reached if earlier stages are skipped. Delegate; support; recognize achievements
5 Adjourning Team reviews lessons learned, documents achievements, celebrates, and formally disbands. Critical for organizational learning and closure. Facilitate closure; capture knowledge; recognize contributions
Critical Point

Storming is the most dangerous stage — many teams never progress beyond it. Skipping any stage (especially Storming or Norming) typically prevents the team from reaching full performance.

3.5 Seven Team Roles (Table 3.1)

RolePrimary Responsibilities & Attributes
SponsorBacker and risk-taker. Approves resource allocation. Believes in the team's concept. Requires business acumen and organizational credibility.
ChampionAdvocate promoting change throughout the organization. Has absolute belief in the initiative. Requires perseverance and persuasive ability.
FacilitatorProcess coach. Observes team interactions, suggests process improvements, defuses conflict. Deals with process — NOT content. Does not report proprietary team information to management.
Team LeaderChange agent. Staffs the team, directs efforts, coaches members, and communicates upward to management. Must be fair, firm, and factual.
TimekeeperMonitors adherence to the meeting agenda. Assertively intervenes when the schedule is threatened.
ScribeRecords minutes and decisions. Must be accurate, organized, and assertive in capturing all relevant discussion.
Team Members (SMEs)Commit to the team's purpose. Express ideas openly, listen attentively, handle stress productively, and bring subject-matter expertise.

3.6 Team Processes and Facilitation (BoK I.C.3–4)

Process Types

Task-Type Processes

  • Keep the team focused and moving toward goals
  • Agenda review and preparation
  • Action item definition and tracking
  • Decision-making technique selection

Maintenance-Type Processes

  • Build interpersonal relationships
  • Preserve team effectiveness over time
  • Role assignment and clarification
  • Conflict de-escalation

Consensus Decision-Making

Consensus requires that all members: (1) objectively listen to all perspectives, (2) openly express their own views, and (3) agree that the final decision is acceptable — even if not their first preference. This is not majority rule. Consensus is more thorough but more time-consuming than voting.

Facilitator Responsibilities

  • Cultivate an unbiased, psychologically safe environment
  • Keep discussion focused on the team's mission
  • Encourage diverse and dissenting viewpoints
  • Regulate interruptions and prevent monopolization
  • Defuse destructive behavior
  • Track ideas visually (whiteboard, flip chart)
  • Help discussions reach actionable closure

3.7 Groupthink and Performance (BoK I.C.5)

Definition

Groupthink occurs when members coalesce around an idea without fully examining it. Members may privately disagree but remain silent to preserve group harmony.

Prevention Strategies

  • Designate a formal devil's advocate role
  • Brainstorm alternatives before evaluating any option
  • Explicitly encourage open dissent
  • Ensure all ideas receive examination before selection

Performance Metrics

Metric TypeExamples
Process metricsProcess yield, first-time quality rate
Resource metricsPerson-hours invested per improvement
Outcome metricsCustomer satisfaction scores
Engagement metricsMeeting attendance and participation rates

Recognition

Monetary: bonuses, gain-sharing, pay-for-skills programs. Non-monetary: certificates, plaques, newsletter features, public recognition events. Teams should have input into defining their own recognition standards within established parameters. Covey's reinforcement principles: Reinforce, Request information, Responsibility, Role model, Repeat.
