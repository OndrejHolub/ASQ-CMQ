§2   Leadership

Summary

Leadership encompasses the theories, styles, and competencies through which individuals influence others toward shared goals. This chapter covers the leader-vs.-manager distinction, leadership styles, emotional intelligence, organizational culture, change management, motivation theory, conflict resolution, and empowerment.

2.1 Leader vs. Manager

DimensionLeaderManager
Source of influenceCharacter, trust, personal power — voluntarily followedFormally designated authority, granted from above
Position required?No — one can lead without a titleYes — officially appointed
Focus (Covey)"Doing the right things""Doing things right"
Deming's view"The job of management is not supervision, but leadership."
Gallup 2005–2008

Four attributes followers most value in leaders: Trust, Compassion, Stability, Hope.

Key roles a leader performs: Enabler, Follower, Advocate, Appraiser, Facilitator.

2.2 Leadership Styles

StyleCharacteristicsKey Association
Cause Leadership Gathers followers around a common goal through personal motivation and power. Uses charisma, empathy, trust, empowerment, ethics, and experience. Highly visible. Multidimensional; closest to "inspirational"
Transactional Clear task-and-reward structure; contractual relationship. Follows defined procedures. Rewards and punishes based on performance. "Hired gun" model. Structured, compliance-driven environments
Transformational Articulates vision and values; elevates goals and self-confidence of followers. Inspires beyond self-interest. Bob Galvin at Motorola
Situational (Hersey-Blanchard) Style varies by task behavior, relationship behavior, and follower readiness/maturity. Four modes: Telling, Selling, Participating, Delegating. Adaptive leadership model

Hersey-Blanchard: Four Situational Styles

StyleTask BehaviorRelationship BehaviorWhen to Use

Selling (S2)HighHighFollower gaining competence but lacks confidence
Participating (S3)LowHighFollower capable but lacks motivation

2.4 Organizational Culture (Schein)

Edgar Schein defined culture as shared basic assumptions learned by a group, considered valid, and taught to new members as the correct way to perceive, think, and feel.

Level 1 — Artifacts
Visible structures, processes, symbols, stories, and myths. The surface layer of culture.
Level 2 — Espoused Values
Stated strategies, goals, and philosophies. What the organization says it believes.
Level 3 — Underlying Assumptions
Unconscious beliefs taken for granted. The deepest and most influential layer. Must be understood before any change initiative.
Regional Emphasis Note

North America: strategic planning emphasis. Europe: benchmarking. South America: strategic planning combined with outsourcing. Culture varies significantly by region; the exam may test these distinctions.

2.5 Change Management

Kotter's 8-Step Model

StepAction
1Create a sense of urgency for the need to change
2Form a guiding coalition with sufficient authority
3Develop a vision and strategy for change
4Communicate the vision broadly and repeatedly
5Empower broad-based action; remove obstacles
6Generate short-term wins and make them visible
7Consolidate gains and produce more change
8Anchor new approaches in organizational culture

Sources of Resistance to Change

  • Comfort with the current condition (status quo bias)
  • Change communicated to wrong people, by wrong method, at wrong time
  • Fear of failure or fear of the unknown
  • Perceived additional workload
  • Poor history of past change initiatives
Senge's Boiling Frog

Gradual change goes unnoticed until the situation becomes critical — like a frog that does not notice slowly rising water temperature. Organizations must develop sensitivity to incremental environmental shifts.

Change Agents

Internal Agents

  • Deep organizational knowledge
  • Existing relationships and trust
  • Vested interest in outcomes
  • Risk: may lack objectivity

External Agents

  • Fresh, unbiased perspective
  • Freedom from internal politics
  • Broad cross-industry experience
  • Risk: unfamiliar with culture

2.6 Motivation Theories

Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

Five levels must be satisfied in sequence from bottom to top. Loss of a job immediately returns a person to the physiological and safety levels, regardless of prior attainment.

LevelNeedWorkplace Relevance
5 (Highest)Self-actualizationMeaningful work, growth, creativity
4EsteemRecognition, status, achievement
3Belonging / SocialTeam membership, relationships
2SafetyJob security, safe conditions
1 (Lowest)PhysiologicalAdequate pay, rest, working conditions

Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory

Motivators (Satisfiers)

  • Achievement
  • Recognition
  • The work itself
  • Responsibility
  • Growth and advancement

Hygiene Factors (Dissatisfiers)

  • Salary and compensation
  • Working conditions
  • Company policy
  • Supervision quality
  • Interpersonal relationships
Critical Distinction

Removing hygiene factors (dissatisfiers) does not create motivation — it only eliminates dissatisfaction. Motivation requires the presence of motivators (satisfiers).

Vroom's Expectancy Theory

Motivation = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence. People act on what they believe will lead to valued rewards. All three components must be present for motivation to occur.

Equity Theory

Individuals compare their own input/output ratio to that of comparable others. Perceived inequity creates tension that motivates behavior to restore balance.

Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Motivation

TypeSourceExamples
IntrinsicFrom the work itselfSatisfaction, achievement, autonomy, creativity, personal growth
ExtrinsicFrom external rewardsPay, bonuses, recognition programs, promotions

Both types coexist within individuals and are not mutually exclusive.

2.7 Conflict Resolution

The Thomas-Kilmann model maps five conflict-handling modes on axes of assertiveness (concern for self) and cooperativeness (concern for others).

ModeAssertivenessCooperativenessOutcome
CompetingHighLowWin-lose
CollaboratingHighHighWin-win — preferred in quality contexts
CompromisingMediumMediumPartial satisfaction for both parties
AccommodatingLowHighYield to others
AvoidingLowLowWithdraw; issue unresolved

Principled negotiation principles: separate people from the problem; focus on interests rather than positions; invent options for mutual gain; use objective criteria.

2.8 Empowerment

Empowerment is the conferral of the right to make decisions and take action. It represents a fundamental culture shift, not merely delegation.

Traditional Structure

  • Manager at the top
  • Employees at the bottom
  • Customers outside the organization

Empowered Structure

  • Customers at the top
  • Employees serve customers directly
  • Manager supports from below (inverted pyramid)

Requirements for successful empowerment: clear boundaries, appropriate training, mutual trust, accountability, and aligned reward systems.

Common failure modes: managers unwilling to relinquish control; unclear decision boundaries; inadequate preparation of employees.

Drucker's Four Management Tasks

Economic performance — Making work productive — Managing social impacts — Managing within time.
